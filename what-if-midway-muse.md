Nếu Muse đọc lệch giọng giữa các đoạn, hãy tắt tiếng các đoạn video và lồng một bản giọng đọc liền mạch tạo bằng công cụ chuyển văn bản thành giọng nói, dùng nguyên văn dưới đây.
```text
One slip of paper decided the biggest gamble of the Pacific war. What if nobody had read it?
June 1942. Six months after Pearl Harbor, the Japanese fleet dominates the Pacific, and plans one more knockout blow.
The target is a tiny atoll called Midway. The real goal is to lure the last American carriers into a trap.
But in a windowless basement at Pearl Harbor, American codebreakers are reading fragments of Japanese naval messages.
Meet Eddie. He is fictional, a young clerk we invented to show you this room. The room itself was real.
The messages keep naming a target called A-F. Nobody can prove where A-F is.
So the codebreakers set a trap of their own. Midway radios, in plain English, that its fresh-water plant has broken down.
Days later, an intercepted Japanese message reports that A-F is short of water. A-F is Midway.
Now the Americans know where, and roughly when. Three carriers slip into position, one of them patched up in about three days.
On June fourth, American dive bombers catch the Japanese carriers at the worst possible moment.
By the end of the battle, all four Japanese carriers are lost. The balance of the Pacific shifts in a single morning.
That is the history. Now, change one thing.
What if the basement was ignored? In real life, some officers in Washington doubted the codebreakers and expected the attack somewhere else.
In our version, Eddie carries the slip upstairs, and it lands in a tray marked unconfirmed.
So the American carriers stay near Hawaii, guarding against the wrong threat.
June fourth. Japanese aircraft strike Midway. This time, no American carriers are waiting in ambush.
Next comes the landing. And here, historians disagree.
Some argue it would have failed anyway. The reef was wide, the defenders were dug in, and the landing force was small.
But let us give the attackers their best possible day. Midway falls.
The American carriers arrive late and fight on Japan's terms. In our scenario, they lose two ships and withdraw.
Japan has won its decisive battle. Its fleet is intact. On paper, this is everything the plan asked for.
So what does Japan do with it?
Midway lies more than a thousand miles from Hawaii. Every bullet and every drum of fuel must cross open ocean to reach it.
Japan was already short of tankers and cargo ships. Holding one more distant island makes that shortage worse.
Invade Hawaii next? Japanese planners studied the idea, and the army was unwilling to spare the troops.
Meanwhile, Eddie's basement does not close. The codebreakers keep working, with more people and more machines.
And across America, something no fleet can sink is speeding up.
Here is the twist. Midway was never going to decide the war. The factories were.
New American carriers, ordered before Pearl Harbor, begin arriving in 1943, in numbers Japan cannot match.
Japan's own fleet commander is said to have warned of this: six months to a year of victories, and no promises after that.
So in our timeline, the Pacific war probably lasts longer. Perhaps a year. Perhaps more.
That is not a small change. A longer war means more people, on every side, who never come home.
But the ending probably stays the same. A battle can be won by surprise. A long war is won by production.
And the surprise at Midway came from a basement, from people almost nobody saluted.
So what is the lesson? The loudest voice in the room is not always the best informed.
Every workplace has an Eddie: someone quiet, holding a slip of paper that matters.
Whether anyone reads it is the one decision that changes everything.
Back in the basement, the slip of paper is still on the desk. This time, someone picks it up.
Which decision in history would you change? Tell me in the comments. Next time: what if the D-Day weather forecast was wrong?
```

# 5. Chữ trên hình, nguồn dẫn và mô tả

## 5.1 Chữ chèn
- Thẻ **HISTORY** (giấy cũ màu kem, tem đỏ) và **WHAT IF** (tông xanh lạnh) đặt ở góc trên bên trái, giữ khoảng 2 giây.
- Dòng **"Source: …"** chữ nhỏ ở góc dưới bên phải, giữ 3–4 giây. Chữ phải đủ to để đọc được trên TV.
